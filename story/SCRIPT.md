## 1. Full article

Page: `story/index.html`.

**PHIRE**: POLAR Henry Ice Rise Expedition

*Radar found what look like crevasses buried deep inside an Antarctic ice rise. They may record how the West Antarctic Ice Sheet shrank and then grew back after the last ice age. PHIRE will drill through them to the bed.*

### Opening

In January 2014 a small team towing an ice-penetrating radar across a remote dome of ice in the Weddell Sea saw something strange in the data: bright, steeply tilted echoes near the bottom of the ice, hundreds of metres beneath them.

A decade later we think those echoes record how this part of Antarctica changed over the last 12,000 years. This is how we found them, and why we are going back to drill through them.

### The ice sheet

The West Antarctic Ice Sheet rests on a bed that lies mostly below sea level. Where its ice reaches the coast it lifts off the bed and floats, forming ice shelves. The grounding line marks where the ice starts to float.

To predict how fast the ice sheet could shrink as the climate warms, we need to know how it behaved in the past, especially during the warm millennia since the last ice age.

### Henry Ice Rise

Ice from the heart of West Antarctica drains into the Ronne Ice Shelf, which flows at hundreds of metres a year (colour shows today's speed).

In the middle of the shelf sits **Henry Ice Rise**: 7,000 km² of ice resting on the seabed, 300 to 800 m below sea level. The shelf flows around it. The ice rise itself moves less than 10 m a year, and its base is frozen to the bed.

### The 2013/14 survey

In the 2013/14 field season we surveyed the ice rise with DELORES, the British Antarctic Survey's deep-looking radio-echo sounder. It sends radio pulses down through the ice and records echoes from layers of buried snow and from the bed below.

The lines appear in the order we drove them, timed from the radar's own logs.

### The discovery

On 9 January, the last day of the survey, we extended it north along the ridge of the ice rise, 37 km to its north-west corner. Most of that radargram is calm: layers of old snowfall, bent by the slow flow of the ice, above the bright echo of the bed.

But in the last 5 km, as we reached the corner, the deepest ice filled with bright, steeply tilted echoes, rising from the bed into the ice above.

### The mystery

They looked like crevasses: cracks that open where ice is pulled apart, usually where it flows fast.

Henry Ice Rise barely moves and is frozen to its bed. So what were crevasses doing hundreds of metres down inside it, and why only near the bottom, beneath smooth, unbroken layers?

### The 2014/15 survey

That corner is why we came back. After finishing the 2013/14 survey, we returned the next summer and, over a week in December 2014, drove a dense grid of lines a few hundred metres apart around it, at the northern end of the ice rise, where the bed is highest and the features come closest to the surface.

### Line FL5

Processed to put each echo back where it came from, line FL5 (driven on 30 December) shows them clearly: steep reflectors that look like cracks, rising from the bed and stopping beneath a smooth layer.

### Where the crevasses are

Line by line, the survey shows where they are. The buried crevasses lie beneath the northern tip and along both flanks of the ice rise, but not in a band down its middle. The points mark where they begin along each line, and the edges of the crevasse-free band follow faint changes in surface texture that run parallel to today's grounding line.

### Crevasses today

We know where cracks like these form today. Where a floating ice shelf runs aground on a high in the seabed, it buckles and stretches and crevasses open, as they do at the nearby Doake Ice Rumples.

This satellite image shows the same thing happening right now at the northern edge of Henry Ice Rise, a few kilometres from our survey: a field of crevasses about a kilometre across.

### A hypothesis

That suggests an explanation. Perhaps Henry Ice Rise was once part of the floating ice shelf. When the shelf ran aground on the seabed high beneath its northern end, it cracked like Doake Ice Rumples does today. Sea water filled the cracks and froze. The ice thickened, stopped flowing, froze to its bed, and centuries of snowfall buried the crevasses.

If so, the grounding line was once far inland of where it is today.

### The ice-sheet model

We explored this with an ice-sheet model (PISM; Kingslake et al., 2018). As the climate warms after the last ice age, the modelled grounding line retreats inland of its present position, by up to 300 km in places. In the Weddell Sea it is furthest inland about 10,200 years ago (blue), and Henry Ice Rise floats off its bed.

Relieved of the weight of ice, the land rebounds and the seabed rises. The shelf runs aground on highs like the one beneath Henry Ice Rise, and the ice sheet grows back towards its present size. (At the model's 15 km resolution only part of the ice rise regrounds.)

### Why it matters

Two histories fit what we know. In one, the ice sheet shrank steadily to its present size and Henry Ice Rise simply slowed down. In the other, the ice sheet retreated much further and then **re-advanced**.

The difference matters for the future. If rebound of the solid Earth halted the retreat, similar processes might slow future ice loss. If the climate drove it, this sector is sensitive to climate. Both histories can explain an ice rise that once flowed faster. Only re-advance predicts frozen sea water inside those crevasses.

### The drill site

So we are going to look. The PHIRE drill site is on line FL5 at the northern end of Henry Ice Rise (79.90° S, 60.25° W), close to the corner where we first saw the buried crevasses, and above the shallowest of them.

### Drilling to the bed

In the 2028/29 season a hot-water drill will melt a hole through the full 550 m of ice to the bed, passing through the buried crevasses about 450 m down.

A camera lowered into the hole will look for the blue streaks of refrozen sea water in its walls, and a corer will sample the sediment beneath, which would hold marine microfossils if the ocean was once there. Radiocarbon dating of that sediment will tell us when the ocean was last there.

### Temperature

A fibre-optic cable left in the hole will measure the temperature of the ice from top to bottom. When floating ice runs aground, the ocean stops warming its base and the ice cools from the bottom up, over thousands of years. The profile measured today still carries the signature of when that happened. With the sediment beneath, it will tell us when the ice rise ran aground.

The curves are from our model of a 500 m column of ice after grounding, with placeholder values for the site.

### Closing

Frozen sea water in the crevasses would show that Henry Ice Rise was once afloat, and that the ice sheet retreated past it and grew back. The temperature of the ice and the sediment beneath it will tell us when, and help pin down why.
